Three sections are scored: Verbal, Quantitative (two math sections combined), and Reading. Your score report also shows percentiles, which compare you with students in your grade who took the test in the last three years. Schools usually care most about percentiles. Scores reach your schools about two to three weeks after the test, along with a copy of your writing sample, and stay active for one academic year. You can take it on paper, at a Prometric test center, or at home with a live proctor.

Test day, in order

Writing sample

1 essay, 25 min, not scored

You choose one of two prompts: a personal one about your own life or opinions, or a general essay or argument prompt. It isn't scored, but a copy goes to every school, so write neatly and proofread.

Quantitative 1

25 questions, 30 min

Arithmetic, algebra, geometry, and data. No calculator.

Reading

40 questions, 40 min

About 8 or 9 passages (fiction, nonfiction, and poetry) with main idea, detail, inference, vocabulary-in-context, tone, and purpose questions. Vocabulary helps here too.

Verbal

60 questions, 30 min

30 synonym questions, then 30 analogy questions, about 30 seconds each. This is the section this app trains directly, and the fastest section to improve with steady practice.

Quantitative 2

25 questions, 30 min

Same kind of math as Quantitative 1.

Experimental

16 questions, 15 min, not scored

Trial questions the test makers are trying out: 6 verbal, 5 math, and 5 reading. They don't count, but they look like real questions, so do your best and keep your energy up.

Scoring: when to guess and when to skip

Each right answer is worth 1 point, each wrong answer costs ¼ point, and a skipped question is 0. Every question has five choices.

  • Blind guess: skip it. With five choices you're right 1 time in 5. On average that earns nothing, because the ¼-point losses cancel the gains.
  • You can rule out one choice: guessing is slightly worth it. You're right 1 time in 4, which comes out a little ahead.
  • You can rule out two or more: guess. That clearly pays off.
  • Questions get harder as each part goes on, but every question is worth the same. Don't get stuck: skip a hard one and come back if there's time.
  • On paper, if you skip a question, skip the same line on the answer sheet. Check your place every 10 questions.

Synonyms (30 questions)

What they look likeVerbal

OBSTINATE: (A) obvious (B) stubborn (C) gentle (D) careless (E) timid

A word in capitals and five choices. Pick the one closest in meaning. The first questions use everyday words; the last ones use hard words, and the choices get harder too.

  • Predict first. Cover the choices and say your own synonym before you look. Then pick the closest match. This stops look-alike choices from pulling you in.
  • Know the word? Answer in under 15 seconds. Spend the saved time on the harder synonyms at the end and on analogies.
  • Half know it? Think of a phrase where you've heard it ("render a verdict", "a fervent fan") and test each choice in that phrase.
  • Don't know it? Break it into parts (see Word roots below) and decide whether it sounds positive or negative. Malevolent starts with mal- (bad), so cross out every positive choice.
  • Watch for traps: words that sound alike (frugal and fragile), choices that are opposites (the test sometimes includes the antonym), and choices that are only loosely related (apiary relates to honey but means a place where bees are kept).
  • Secondary meanings appear. Husband can mean "to conserve", list can mean "to tilt", founder can mean "to sink". If every choice looks wrong, think of another meaning.

Analogies (30 questions)

Format 1: complete the pairVerbal

Frugal is to thrifty as verbose is to: (A) concise (B) honest (C) wordy (D) careful (E) silent

Answer: (C). Frugal means the same as thrifty, so you need the word that means the same as verbose.

Format 2: pick the matching pairVerbal

Petal is to flower as: (A) car is to wheel (B) chapter is to book (C) classroom is to desk (D) author is to essay (E) library is to shelf

Answer: (B). A petal is part of a flower; a chapter is part of a book. (A) and (C) put the whole first: the reversed-order trap.

The bridge-sentence method

  • 1. Make a bridge. Write a short sentence that links the first two words using a definition: "A petal is one part of a flower." Avoid weak bridges like "a petal is on a flower", which fit too many choices.
  • 2. Test every choice by plugging it into the same sentence. Exactly one should fit.
  • 3. If two fit, make the bridge more specific. "Chuckle is to laugh": both are sounds of amusement, but more precisely, a chuckle is a quiet, small laugh.
  • 4. Can't build a bridge? Go the other way: make a bridge for each choice and see which one could also fit the first pair. You can often eliminate pairs with no clear relationship (like "library is to shelf").

The relationship types

Each one is a card in your Analogies list, with practice questions.

The most common types

  • Synonyms: prevaricate : lie   Antonyms: debilitate : strengthen
  • Degree: drip : deluge (mild to extreme)   Part to whole: stanza : poem
  • Type and category: penicillin : antibiotic   Group and member: wolf : pack
  • Tool and use: hammer : pound   Worker and tool: surgeon : scalpel
  • Worker and product: composer : score   Location: silo : grain
  • Cause and effect: discontent : rebellion   Characteristic: stale : bread
  • Lack: stoic : emotion   Sequence and stages: student : graduate

Also common on Upper Level tests

  • Measuring instruments: barometer : pressure   Field of study: pathology : disease
  • Units of measure: pint : quart   Shapes: cylinder : can
  • Raw material and product: wheat : bread   Symbol: dove : peace
  • Covers or worn on: scarf : neck   Sound: patter : rain
  • Removes or prevents: antidote : poison   Manner: scrawl : write
  • Leader and group: captain : crew   Plan and purpose: agenda : meeting
  • Person and action: miser : hoard   Secondary meanings: table : postpone

Analogy traps

  • Reversed order: the right relationship, but backward (wheel : car when you need part : whole).
  • "Both are" pairs: two members of the same group (apple : orange) when the stem is "member : group".
  • Same topic, wrong relationship: a choice about bread when the stem is about a baker, but the relationship is different.
  • Synonym lure: in an antonym analogy, a choice that means the same as the third word.
  • Right relationship, wrong strength: in a degree analogy (drip : deluge), a pair that is related but not mild-to-extreme, such as two words of equal strength.
  • Wrong part of speech: if the stem is noun : verb (hammer : pound), the answer usually is too.

Pacing in the Verbal section

  • 60 questions in 30 minutes is about 30 seconds a question.
  • A useful target: the first 15 synonyms in about 6 minutes, the last 15 in about 7, leaving about 17 minutes for the 30 analogies, which take longer.
  • Use the Timed practice tab: start with Quick practice, then move to the full section in the last few weeks.

Word roots that unlock the most words

A small set of roots shows up again and again in SSAT words. Your Word roots list has 69 of them. A few with the biggest payoff:

  • bene (good): benevolent, benign   mal (bad): malevolent, malicious
  • loqu (speak): loquacious, eloquent, circumlocution   circum (around)
  • chron (time): chronic, anachronism   path (feeling): apathy, empathy, antipathy
  • vert (turn): invert, subvert, introvert   cred (believe): credulity
  • omni (all): omniscient, omnipotent   somn (sleep): somnolent, somnambulist

A study plan that works

  • Free practice tests: CrackSSAT.com has free synonym, analogy, reading, and math practice sets by level. Use the Upper Level ones.
  • Daily: one Study session. Ten new words and the reviews that are due takes 10 to 20 minutes. Short and daily beats long and occasional.
  • Weekly: one timed practice. Send missed words to your reviews from the results page.
  • After every practice test (from the official SSAT practice book or your tutor), log each missed question in the Mistakes tab so you can see what to fix.
  • Read every day. Articles, novels, and science writing build the vocabulary and reading speed that help on both Verbal and Reading.
  • Get the official practice material from the test maker at ssat.org. Real questions are the best final check.
