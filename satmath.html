What's tested

Algebra

about 35%, 13–15 questions

Linear equations, inequalities, and systems; linear functions and their graphs; writing equations from word problems.

Advanced Math

about 35%, 13–15 questions

Quadratics, exponents and radicals, exponential growth and decay, polynomials, rational equations, and function notation.

Problem-Solving and Data Analysis

about 15%, 5–7 questions

Percents, ratios, rates, unit conversions, statistics, probability, and reading tables and graphs.

Geometry and Trigonometry

about 15%, 5–7 questions

Area and volume, lines and angles, triangles, circles, and right-triangle trigonometry.

The most common question types

An analysis of the official Bluebook practice tests found these come up again and again. Each one has cards in Lexiloop Math.

  • Intercepts and slope: plugging in 0, the slope formula, and the slope and intercepts of Ax + By = C.
  • Systems of equations, including when there is no solution or infinitely many.
  • Exponential growth and decay: reading the starting value and the rate, and the y-intercept of an exponential graph.
  • Percents: increase, decrease, markup, and discount.
  • Factoring and quadratics: difference of squares, factor by grouping, the vertex, and the discriminant.
  • What a number in an equation means in a word problem, usually a slope or an intercept.
  • Matching coefficients when two expressions are equivalent.
  • Answers that aren't x: solving for x when the question asks for something like 2x − 3.
  • Geometry basics: angle facts, SOH-CAH-TOA, similar triangles, circle equations, and radians.
  • Unit conversions, especially with squared or cubed units.

What the official Question Bank shows

The College Board's free SAT Suite Question Bank has real math questions from every topic and difficulty level. A review of 1,925 of them shows what the test actually asks, beyond the formulas themselves.

  • About a quarter are type-in questions (464 of 1,925), with no answer choices to check against. Plug your answer back in before moving on.
  • About 1 in 12 has an unknown constant, like "where k is a constant." These ask what k must be for no solution, exactly one solution, an equivalent expression, or a graph that touches a line once.
  • About 1 in 10 is set in the xy-plane, and a similar share includes a graph to read. Reading slopes, intercepts, intersections, and shaded regions from a graph is as important as algebra.
  • About 1 in 8 asks "which equation" or "which expression" represents a situation. Practice translating words into equations, not just solving them.
  • Statistics questions lean on concepts, not calculation: how an added value changes the mean and median, why skewed data pull the mean, what happens to the standard deviation when every value shifts, and who a survey's results apply to.
  • Basic skills still show up often: factoring out a common factor, subtracting polynomials, rectangle perimeter, and solving a formula for one variable. They're easy points if you don't rush.

Cards for topics found in the Question Bank carry a "Question Bank" badge. In Browse, choose Question Bank to see them.

Question families that keep coming back

Comparing recaps of the official r/SAT threads from November 2024 through September 2026 shows the same kinds of questions returning, sometimes years apart and with new numbers. Each has cards in Lexiloop Math.

  • Matching coefficients with x⁴: expressions like 6x⁴ + 59x² + 19 = (3x² + a)(2x² + b) appeared in November 2024, June 2025, and November 2025. Treat x² as one variable.
  • Slant height on pyramids and cones: November 2024 and September 2026, plus a cone question that repeated from March 2025.
  • Function symmetry: August 2024 and August 2026.
  • Flat fee plus "each additional" unit: September 2025 and August 2026. The first unit is already covered, so the expression uses (x − 1).
  • Mean versus median with an outlier, and average rate of change: November 2024, and statistics like these appear on nearly every test.
  • Number of solutions (none, one, or infinitely many) for systems, linear equations, and absolute value equations: in several 2025 and 2026 recaps.
  • Easy questions in the hard module: on the September 2025 international test, a hard Module 2 question needed only the fact that a triangle's angles add to 180°, and students overthought it. Not every question in Module 2 is a trick.

What students learned on recent tests

After every SAT, thousands of students on r/Sat reconstruct the questions from memory. These lessons come from the official discussion threads for the August, September, and October 2026 tests and the follow-up analyses that checked students' answers:

  • Late in a hard Module 2, expect "constant questions." The answer is an expression like a + 6, or a statement like "c cannot be −5," not a number. These include questions about exactly one solution, no solution, or infinitely many. Desmos helps less here, so know the rules cold.
  • Slant height is the biggest geometry trap. For the surface area of a cone or pyramid, use the slant height, not the vertical height. Use the vertical height for volume.
  • "Percent increase" isn't "percent of." Growing from 40 to 100 is 250% of the original, but a 150% increase.
  • Read the question's last few words. Grams of solution or grams of salt? The value of p or of 2p? Several of the most-argued questions were lost at the very last step.
  • Don't round unless the question says to. A mean of 4.24 eggs is entered as 4.24, even though you can't have part of an egg.
  • Find the base in percent questions. "What percent of a is b" means b ÷ a. If the choices include a number and its reciprocal, like 3000% and 3.33%, the question is testing exactly this.
  • Test your expression with a real number. For a fee or pricing formula, plug in a small value you can check by hand. It takes five seconds and catches most setup mistakes.
  • Look for symmetry before doing algebra. On one hard August question, every x sat inside (x + 4)², so the answer came from pairing up inputs, not from solving for three unknown constants.
  • Watch for partial answers. A common trap is a choice that matches one step of the solution, like the radius when the question asks for the diameter.
  • Convert time units inside the exponent. On the October 2026 US test, bacteria doubled every 67 minutes, with time in hours. The exponent is 60x/67, not 67x/60. Check by plugging in one doubling period: the exponent must come out to 1.
  • Know the hemisphere and the radius-from-circumference step. October 2026 had a hemisphere volume, ⅔πr³, and a solid where only the base's circumference was given, so you find r = C ÷ 2π first.
  • Use complementary angles in right-triangle trig. With acute angles r and s, sin r = cos s, so sin r ÷ cos s = 1, while sin r ÷ sin s = tan r, which is bigger than 1 when r is the larger angle.
  • Questions get reused. On the August test, several students recognized a hard Module 2 question from Bluebook Practice Test 8. Working through every official practice test and the Question Bank gives you a real chance of seeing something familiar.
  • Different students get different questions. Two people on the same module see different items, so you can't tell which module you got from one question.

What the reference sheet gives you

During both math modules, you can open a reference sheet with these formulas and facts. Everything else in Lexiloop Math, which covers most of the test, you need to know from memory.

Typing your own answer

About a quarter of the math questions have no answer choices; you type the answer instead. These are the official rules:

  • Negative answers are allowed. You can enter up to 5 characters for a positive answer and up to 6 for a negative one, including the minus sign. (Some older cheat sheets say otherwise. They're wrong.)
  • No mixed numbers. Enter 3½ as 7/2 or 3.5, never 3 1/2.
  • Long decimals: if a decimal doesn't fit, cut it off or round it at the fourth digit. For 2/3, enter 2/3, .6666, or .6667, but not .67.
  • No symbols: leave out percent signs, commas, and dollar signs.
  • More than one correct answer? Enter just one.
  • Don't round on your own. Enter the exact value unless the question tells you to round.

What you can skip

Older SAT cheat sheets, like the handwritten ones that circulate online, include topics the current test doesn't cover. You don't need to study:

  • Logarithms, matrices, and vectors
  • The law of sines, the law of cosines, trig identities, and the unit circle beyond 30°, 45°, and 60°
  • Ellipses, hyperbolas, and polar coordinates
  • Permutations, combinations, and the binomial theorem
  • Complex numbers and the polynomial remainder theorem, which essentially disappeared from the digital SAT

Using Desmos well

  • Solve any equation by graphing: type each side as its own line (y = left side and y = right side). The x-value where they cross is the answer.
  • Find a vertex or zeros: graph the function and tap the points Desmos highlights.
  • Check answer choices: plug each one in to see which works.
  • Unknown constants: when an equation has a constant like k, type it with the constant; Desmos offers a slider. Drag it to see when the graphs touch, cross, or never meet.
  • Statistics: type a list like L = [3, 9, 10, 27], then mean(L), median(L), or stdev(L).

Desmos is fast, but knowing the formulas still matters. Many questions ask what a number in an equation means, which a graph can't tell you.

Test-day habits

  • Read exactly what's asked. Many wrong answers come from solving for x when the question wants 2x, or another quantity.
  • Keep moving at about a minute and a half per question. Flag hard ones and come back.
  • Take Module 1 seriously, since it decides how high your score can go in Module 2.
  • If a typed answer comes out negative, enter it. Negative answers are allowed.
